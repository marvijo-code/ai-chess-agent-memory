## Opening pattern

## The decisive error
- After ...Rfe8 and c3, I played ...Re2?! to attack b2. Bf3 defended e2, but White's Bd1! attacked the rook with a cheaper piece. A defended rook is not immune to a bishop attack.
- I spent 59 seconds on ...Be4?? and claimed it retained support of Re2. It did not: from e4 the bishop reaches d3/c2/b1 and f3/g2/h1, not e2.
- Bxe2 won an entire rook. No bishop was captured in return. White retained both bishops and both rooks against my two bishops and one rook. Calling this an exchange sacrifice concealed the material loss.
- Before accepting any capture: place the capturing piece on its destination, name my recapturer, trace the complete path, then count both sides' final material. No legal recapture means a full piece sacrifice.
- After Bd1, compare legal rook retreats such as ...Re7 and ...Re8 before trying to preserve the invasion. These are candidates, not engine-verified best moves. Earlier, compare ordinary development with ...Re2; reaching rank two is not automatically progress.

## Resistance and limits
- The supported c-pawn reached c2 and tied a rook to c1. Exchanging its bishop guard on e4 left it vulnerable; White eventually took it. The new e-passer reached e2 with king support but fell to coordinated rooks. Name a concrete way to dislodge a blockader before claiming promotion threats.
- Rg4 was supported but lacked mobility against White's f4/g3 pawns and bishop. h3 eventually forced ...Rxg5, giving R for B. Check escape squares as well as defenders before advancing a rook into a pawn cage.
- The b-passer later promoted with king support and bought a rook after White captured the new queen. The resulting R+3P versus B was still lost. Recount after promotion; do not equate promotion with recovery.
- SF broke a checking cycle by pushing h6. A past repetition escape is no reason to expect another while it can advance pawns.
